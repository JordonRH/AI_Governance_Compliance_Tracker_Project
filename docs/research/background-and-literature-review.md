# AI governance in higher education: background and literature review

Status: Candidate research baseline for team review
Prepared: 20 September 2026
Scope: Background work for GitHub issue #3; not a completed research paper or sponsor-approved policy position.

## Research focus

AITrace investigates how general AI governance principles can be translated into a practical register-assess-track-report workflow for a higher-education institution. The project does not create a legal compliance standard and does not assume that registration means approval or compliance.

The proposal identifies three use contexts: education, administration and research. These contexts share governance concerns but create different impacts, stakeholders and evidence needs. A useful institutional workflow must therefore record the intended use and context before applying any later assessment process.

## Why institutional visibility matters

AI may enter institutional work through centrally approved systems, local experiments or individual use of commercial tools. Without a current register, an institution cannot reliably identify responsible owners, affected people, data use, expected benefits, or the controls and review arrangements attached to each use.

The governance problem extends beyond academic misconduct. Current higher-education literature describes AI governance as a socio-technical problem involving policy, people, institutional processes and technology. Barus et al. identify fragmented governance practice and argue for attention to both governance components and stakeholder relationships. Jiang and Abdullah find that research has focused more heavily on teaching applications than institutional governance and that evidence about transparency, accountability, equity, trust and organisational effects remains limited.

These findings support AITrace's register-first approach. They do not establish which framework, policy or risk threshold the sponsor should adopt.

## Higher-education contexts

### Education

Teaching, learning and assessment raise questions about educational purpose, student agency, equitable access, disclosure, academic integrity, staff capability and assurance of learning. UNESCO recommends a human-centred approach that considers privacy, agency, inclusion, equity and cultural diversity and asks institutions to validate pedagogical and ethical suitability. TEQSA's Australian guidance frames generative-AI assessment reform around assurance of learning while recognising the need to prepare students for environments where AI is common.

For AITrace, an education record may eventually need evidence about the learning purpose, affected cohorts, human oversight, disclosure and applicable assessment policy. The exact fields and review rules require institutional confirmation.

### Administration

Administrative AI may affect service delivery, resource allocation, communications or decisions about people. Relevant concerns include a clear purpose, accountable ownership, personal-information handling, fairness, transparency, contestability, reliability and monitoring.

The OAIC states that privacy obligations may apply to personal information both entered into and generated by an AI product. Its guidance calls for product due diligence, consideration of intended use, human oversight, privacy and security risks, access to information, transparency and applicable Australian Privacy Principles. Whether the Privacy Act 1988 applies to the sponsor and a specific use is a legal/institutional question outside this project.

### Research

Research uses can involve confidential or sensitive information, intellectual contribution, reproducibility, research integrity, publication disclosure and third-party tool terms. UNESCO includes research within its generative-AI guidance and highlights human agency, privacy, inclusion and validation of ethical suitability. NIST AI RMF also treats scientific integrity, data suitability, system context and human oversight as risk-management considerations.

AITrace must not infer research approval from a registry entry. Applicable ethics, integrity, data-management and publication requirements need to come from confirmed institutional sources.

## Recurring governance themes

The reviewed sources converge at a topic level on:

- accountable ownership and governance;
- a documented purpose, context and affected stakeholders;
- benefits, adverse impacts and proportional risk management;
- human agency and meaningful oversight;
- fairness, inclusion and accessibility;
- privacy, data governance and security;
- reliability, safety, testing and monitoring;
- transparency, disclosure and explainability;
- contestability, incident handling and remediation;
- lifecycle review and continuous improvement.

Convergence does not make the sources interchangeable. NIST AI RMF is voluntary and use-case agnostic. Australia's AI Ethics Principles express voluntary principles. ISO/IEC 42001 specifies an organisational AI management system. OAIC guidance addresses Australian privacy considerations. UNESCO and TEQSA provide education-sector context. Institutional policies and applicable law must still be identified.

## Implications for AITrace

The evidence supports a staged workflow:

1. Register an AI use and its responsible owner.
2. Record intended purpose, context, people affected and data involved.
3. Apply an approved, source-mapped assessment.
4. Record an explainable result without equating risk with approval or legal compliance.
5. Assign and track governance actions.
6. Review changes, incidents and continuing suitability.
7. Report status to authorised roles.

Only the first stage and basic context classification currently exist. Later stages require confirmed roles, institutional policies, questions, rules, thresholds and approval terminology.

## Limitations and open questions

- This is a focused baseline, not a systematic review performed by the AITrace team.
- The full text of ISO/IEC 42001 was not available for this review; only ISO's public description was used. Clause-level mapping requires lawful access to the standard.
- NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is under revision. A version must be fixed when assessment rules are approved.
- Higher-education evidence is developing quickly and includes limited longitudinal evidence about institutional governance outcomes.
- Applicability of Australian privacy law and other obligations requires qualified institutional/legal review.
- Sponsor confirmation is still required for roles, policies, framework selection, approval workflow, compliance terminology and priorities.
